# Drawing for tandem

Pixel art as text: draw as text, render to PNG, look at it, iterate. (This way of working, the format and the tools are pixeldraw's.)

## Way of working: silhouette first
Do NOT think for minutes before drawing. Keep thinking to a few sentences: pick a size, palette and pose, then immediately Write the silhouette. Don't plan the whole drawing in your head; let the renders guide you:
Two phases. Within a phase, iterate on your own in rounds (edits, events and render, look; see Loop) until you're happy. After each phase, stop: the user gives feedback before you continue.
1. **silhouette**: write the file with `@size` and `@shapes` only (ellipses, rects, thick lines, polys), in two steps that run on without a stop in between. First the shape, in one tone (`3 = #5a6988`), until the contour is closed and reads; then the depth pass (see the end of this step). Never use circles or ellipses for hands, feet or fingers: use `@poly` with a thumb and fingers wrapped around a grip, feet/boots with sole, heel and toe. Build it from basic shapes: break the subject into simple volumes (head = circle/ellipse, torso = rect or trapezoid poly, limbs = thick lines, weapon/props = rects and polys), one shape line per volume. Get the big shapes right first (overall proportions, head-to-body ratio, gesture line, size of each mass relative to the others), only then add secondary shapes. Then check the joins: shapes must flow into each other (neck into shoulders, arm into torso, hip into legs) without steps, notches, gaps or blobby bulges where two shapes overlap. Where a join looks lumpy or disconnected, refine it: add a small poly to bridge the gap, or erase corners with `.` (a poly, line or `@px`) to shave off bumps, until the contour reads as one smooth form. In each review round, name the basic shapes you see in the render and compare them to what they should be (too long, too wide, wrong angle, missing mass); with a reference image, compare shape by shape against the reference. Fix the shape by editing the shape lines (proportions, pose, readability at 2x) until it reads. This is fast: a few lines instead of thousands of pixels.
   Depth pass: once the shape reads, repaint the shapes in five depth tones, so it's clear which parts are in front and which behind: `1 = #2b3048` (farthest), `2 = #3f4a68`, `3 = #5a6988` (torso and head), `4 = #7d90ab`, `5 = #a6bccf` (nearest). Limbs on the far side go on 1 or 2, limbs on the near side on 4 or 5. Every separate object (weapon, shield, bag, tail) gets its own step, different from the body part that touches it: a hand around a grip is one step nearer than the weapon (e.g. sword on 4, the gripping fingers on 5). Draw in depth order, from 1 to 5, so overlaps are right. Don't change the outer contour in this step, only the tones and the order of the shape lines; announce it as a round ("depth pass").
   When the user approves the silhouette, it is saved as `depth/<name>.txt`: in the color phase, render it with `node scripts/render.js depth/<name>.txt` (→ `out/depth/<name>.png`) to check what is in front and what behind. Keep it in sync with the drawing: the viewer and attachments use it to decide per pixel what is in front. When a part moves, changes depth or the contour changes a lot (in the color phase, or when the user asks for it), Edit the depth map too: same shapes, only the five depth tones, frames and animations matching the art.
   A depth-only project (`@depthonly`, like the testman dummy) is drawn only in the five depth tones: the drawing is its own depth map, so there is no `depth/<name>.txt`, and there is no color phase. The silhouette with its depth pass is the end result; finish it with `phase <name> color`.
2. **color**: the silhouette is a loose guide for pose and proportions, not a mask to color in. Make a real drawing based on it: redraw parts with new shapes where that draws better (shapes, contour and details may deviate from the silhouette), built up in layers. First round: only large flat color areas (one color per big mass, no details). Every next round, choose explicitly and say it in the round event: either improve (fix what's wrong in the current layer) or go further (add the next layer: secondary color areas, then shading, then rough details). Only go further when the current layer reads.
   Two rules for every color round: give each material (coat, hat, skin, wood, stone, metal) its own ramp of 2 to 4 steps, dark to light, distinct from the materials it touches, and shade only within that ramp; and every mark on a surface needs a cause you can name (a seam, a fold, a crack, the grain, wear on an edge, moss where it's damp), in the place that cause would put it. Many such small marks, each a pixel or two, are good: they make a surface read as real and detailed; random noise without a cause is not detail.
   Start the color phase with a short plan of which layer and details come in which step, and send it: `node scripts/event.js plan <name> "base colors | secondary areas | shading | eyes and nose | final check"` (steps separated by `|`, the last one is always `final check`). At every round, send the step you work on: `node scripts/event.js step <name> <n>` (improve = same step, go further = next step). The last round is the final check: check whether everything is right (against the request, the reference and the plan) and whether you forgot anything. If something is off, jump back to the step where it belongs (`step <name> <n>` with that earlier step), fix it and work forward again. Only finish the phase after an final check that passes. (Later shapes paint over earlier ones; `@outline` adds the outer contour; `@px x y chars` for a few exact pixels like eyes). This is the last phase.
Keep shape lines grouped with a `//` comment per part (head, torso, arm, ...) so they stay easy to find and change.

Animations follow the same two phases, per animation:
1. **silhouette** = the motion. Block out every frame with `@shapes`, first in one tone (the motion, timing and the manual pass below), then the depth pass over every frame (see above), in depth order per frame. When a limb crosses the body (an arm swinging forward, a leg passing), its tone stays the same; only its place in the order changes if it really moves to the other side. Key poses first (contact, extremes), then inbetweens, and set the timing with frame durations. Simplify the body to volumes (head, torso, limbs as thick lines, weapon). Check in the render strip that weight, arcs, contact points and overlap read; the user watches it play in the viewer.
   The first blockout is almost always too mechanical. Before finishing the phase, always do a manual pass frame by frame, as an animator would by hand: no parts that just slide or rotate rigidly between poses; uneven spacing (ease in/out around the extremes instead of equal steps); body parts offset in time (hips lead, shoulders/head/tail/hair/weapon follow and overshoot); motion on arcs, not straight lines; squash and stretch on contact and push-off; asymmetry instead of mirrored copies; varied frame durations (hold the key poses, fast inbetweens). Announce it as a round ("manual pass") and name per frame what you loosened. Then stop for the user's feedback.
2. **color**: base colors and rough details per frame, still shapes; keep them consistent across frames and with the existing still/first frame.

Canvas size: default 32x32 or 64x64; at most 128x128. Only go bigger when the user explicitly asks for a size, and then lean even more on shapes and `@px`.
When a phase is done: `node scripts/event.js phase <name> <silhouette|color>`, then stop with a short summary. Work that isn't drawing in phases (a small change to existing drawings, a recolor) ends with `phase <name> color` too.

## Loop
A round is three steps, not more: every step re-reads the whole conversation, so a few full steps cost far less than many small ones.
1. Write/edit `art/<name>.txt`: all edits of the round in one message (several Edit calls at once), not one Edit per step.
2. One Bash call that sends the round's events and renders: `node scripts/event.js feedback <name> "<critique of the last render>" + round <name> "round 3: legs" && node scripts/render.js ...` (see Live events). Render options: `node scripts/render.js art/<name>.txt [scale] [--grid] [--crop=x,y,w,h] [--anim=<name>,...] [--frame=<n>] [--with=...]` → `out/<name>.png` (`--crop` renders only that part of the canvas, to zoom in; `--anim` only those animations, `--frame` only frame n (from 1) of each; `--with` see Attachments). Without `--anim` every animation is rendered, for an attachment twice (alone and on the base), which makes a big, scaled-down image: while working on one animation, render only that one with `--anim`, and render everything only for an overview or the final check.
3. Read `out/<name>.png` once per round, not after every edit (the Read tool shows the image), and critique: silhouette, symmetry, contrast, readability in the small 2x preview at the bottom-left. Every render also writes `out/<name>.grid.txt`: each frame's final char grid (after all shapes) with x/y rulers. For exact fixes (a stray pixel, a seam, a gap between two shapes) read the rows you need from it (Read with offset/limit) instead of guessing coordinates from the PNG, then fix them with `@px` or a shape at exactly those coordinates.
4. Repeat until satisfied


Never use a headless browser for checking; the render script is the review path.
Always draw by hand directly in `art/*.txt`, with shape lines and `@px`. Never write scripts or generators to produce or change art.
For analysis you may write a small script in `scratch/` (e.g. `scratch/holes.mjs`) and run it with `node scripts/scratch.js scratch/holes.mjs [args]`: it can read your files (`art/`, `depth/`, `out/<name>.grid.txt`) and import `../src/parse.js` (`parseGrids(text)` gives every frame's final char grid as `frames[i].chars`), and write only in `scratch/`. Use it to check all frames mechanically (stray pixels, holes, a char enclosed by another, two frames that differ), then fix what it finds by hand.

## Reference image
When the user sends a reference image (`refs/*.png`), add `@ref <path>` right after the palette (next to `@size`); crop with `x y w h` so the subject fills the canvas the way you plan to draw it. Every render then shows the reference next to the art at the same size. In every review, compare the two side by side and describe the differences concretely in the feedback event: proportions and size of each basic shape, pose and angles, position on the canvas, missing or extra parts, and (in the color phase) colors. Then fix the biggest difference first.

## Live events (the user watches)
The person sees what you are doing as a line under your work. Emit these while working; several in one call, separated by `+` (`node scripts/event.js feedback <name> "..." + round <name> "..." + step <name> 3`), together with the render (see Loop):
- `node scripts/event.js round <name> "round 2: goal"` at the start of each iteration
- `node scripts/event.js feedback <name> "<critique>"` after looking at a render
- `node scripts/event.js done <name> "<summary>"` when finished
- `node scripts/event.js plan <name> "step 1 | step 2 | ... | final check"` at the start of the color phase, `node scripts/event.js step <name> <n>` at every color round
Use Write only to create a new project. For changes and feedback, always Edit just the rows that change; never rewrite the whole file.
A request for a new animation on an existing project is drawn in its own draft file `art/<project>--<anim>.txt`: copy the project's palette, `@size` and `@depthonly` if it has one, put the frames under `=== <anim>`, and leave the project file alone. When the user accepts the draft, it is merged into the project as that animation (extra palette colors are added; a project char never changes color, a draft char with another color is renamed in the draft frames).

## Art format
```
// comment
. = none          (transparent; '.' is transparent by default)
k = #1a1c2c       (#rrggbb or #rrggbbaa)

....kkkk....
..kkrrrrkk..
```
Palette lines, one blank line, then grid rows (one char per pixel). Short rows are padded with transparent.

### Shapes (blockout)
Lines starting with `@` draw on the frame, in order, on top of its grid rows. Coordinates are pixels, `c` is a palette char (`.` erases).
```
@size 64 64                  canvas size (needed when a frame has only shapes)
@ref refs/x.png [x y w h]     reference image, shown in an extra column of the render at canvas size; optional crop in source pixels
@base <project>              this project is an attachment of that project (see Attachments)
@depthonly                   a project drawn only in the depth tones, its own depth map (no depth/ file, no color phase)
@tiles w h                   a tilesheet: the canvas is cut into tiles of w x h
@tile <name> col row [cols rows]  names a tile (or a block of tiles), by column and row
@strip <name> <v|h> <start> <middle> <end>  a piece that stretches: start, the middle repeated, end (`-` for none), down (v) or right (h)
@view <how it is seen>       per animation, right after `=== <anim>`: how the body is seen (`3/4, turned slightly to the left`, `profile, walking to the left`)
@recolor <name> [from=<recolor>] c=#hex ...  a color variant: those palette chars get other colors; from= builds on another recolor
@variant [<recolor>]         per frame: the shape lines after it are that recolor's detail layer (alone: back to the shared drawing)
@slot <slot>                 the attachment's slot: skin, back, legs, feet, torso, hands, head, right-hand or left-hand
@pin x y                     in an attachment: makes this frame a pivot image, its point (x, y) placed on the base's anchor
@anchor <slot> x y [depth] [r<deg>]  per frame, in a base: where the pivot image of that slot's attachment goes, optional depth 1-5 and rotation
@rect x y w h c
@ellipse cx cy rx ry c
@circle cx cy r c
@line x1 y1 x2 y2 c [width]
@poly c x1,y1 x2,y2 x3,y3 ...
@fill x y c                  flood fill the area of the same char
@outline c                   contour around everything that's not transparent
@px x y chars                pixels from (x,y) to the right; `_` keeps a pixel, `.` erases
```

### Tilesheets
Pieces that a game puts together at any size (a ladder, a rope, a wall) are drawn once as a tilesheet: `@tiles 16 16` cuts the canvas into tiles, `@tile` names each one, and `@strip` says which tiles make a stretchable piece (`@strip ladder v ladder-top ladder-mid ladder-bottom`). The middle tile must join itself and the start and end without a seam. The render shows the tile borders in magenta and, under the frames, every strip put together at length (start, the middle three times, end): check the joins there. 

### Animation
One file = one project (one image). It can hold several named animations that share palette and size.
Start an animation with `=== <name>`; separate frames with a `---` line, optionally with a duration in ms (default 150).
A `---` right after `===` sets the first frame's duration. Without `===` there is one animation called `main`.
```
=== idle
--- 300
..kk..
.kkkk.
--- 100
......
.kkkk.
=== jump
..kk..
......
```
The render shows one row per animation with its frames side by side; check that consecutive frames line up and the motion reads.
A base (a project others draw attachments for) gives every animation a `@view` line, so whoever draws an item for it knows from which angle to draw it; a new animation on a base gets one too.

## Attachments
An attachment is clothing or gear drawn to fit a base project (e.g. armor on a body), so it animates along with it. It is a project of its own with `@base <project>` and `@slot <slot>` right after the palette, the same `@size`, and the same animations as the base (same names, frame counts and durations): frame i of animation `run` is drawn on frame i of the base's `run`. Read the base file for the pose per frame and draw only the attachment.
Slots: `skin`, `back`, `legs`, `feet`, `torso`, `hands`, `head`, `right-hand`, `left-hand` (left and right as seen from the character; `hands` is gloves, the hand slots hold a weapon, shield or other item). A base wears at most one item per slot (the user picks it in the viewer), so draw only what belongs in your slot. `skin` is a different look of the body itself (skin tone, fur, markings), drawn per frame over the whole body.
Per animation of the base, an attachment is either drawn frame by frame (clothing that deforms with the body; frames under `=== <anim>` with the base's animation name), or shows a pivot image: rigid gear like a helmet or weapon as one image, a frame with `@pin x y`, with (x, y) the point that goes on the anchor (a weapon's grip, the middle of a helmet). The shared pivot image is in a section `=== pivot` (an older pinned attachment is only that image); an animation can have a pivot image of its own, a section named like the base's animation with one frame and its own `@pin`, for a view of its own (a helmet seen from the side while walking). Per animation of the base: frames of its own if it has them, else its own pivot image, else the shared one, wherever the base has `@anchor <slot> x y [depth]` in that frame, on the matching spot of the body (the gripping hand, the head). The user picks frames or pivot (shared or its own) per animation in the viewer. `r<degrees>` turns the image clockwise around its pin (`r-45`, `r90`; draw the image upright, at r0); frames without `r` get the angle interpolated between the nearest frames before and after that have one (the animation loops), so key the extremes and let the inbetweens follow. Values past 360 or below 0 are fine, to turn the long way round. Turned images are sampled RotSprite-style, so lines stay clean, but small details can shift a pixel. A pinned image may stick out of the base's canvas: the composite (viewer, export, render) then grows on that side for all frames; `--crop` and the marks stay in the base's coordinates. The anchor's depth, when given, is the depth of the whole image in that frame, so a sword can swing behind the back (1) and in front of the body (5); without it the image's own depth tones count. When a base gets a new animation that shows a pivot image, its anchors go in every new frame too. Renders mark anchors and the pin with a magenta dot.
The viewer and render composite per pixel by depth: the depth tones of the silhouette (depth maps) say what is nearest, and on equal depth the attachment lies on top, a later slot in the list above over an earlier one. So in the depth pass give an attachment the tone of the body part it sits on (chest armor 3, a sleeve on the near arm 4 or 5), and a farther tone where it belongs behind the body (a cape behind the back 1 or 2).
A `skin` follows the depth of its base: where the base has a pixel, the skin gets the base's depth, so changes to the base and new animations carry over to every skin. Its depth map `depth/<name>.txt` holds only what the base doesn't define: where the skin differs from the base (hair or a ponytail behind the back on 1) and where it is outside the body; over the body it stays empty, also in frames without any override (an empty frame is just its `---` line). It is not saved from the silhouette: write it yourself when there is something to override, with the same animations and frame count as the skin. Every skin pixel outside the body (hair, ears, a wider build) must have a pixel in that depth map. `node scripts/render.js depth/<skin>.txt` (also before the file exists) renders the depth the skin gets: see-through what it takes from the base, solid its own depth map, red where a pixel outside the body has no depth; `out/depth/<skin>.grid.txt` shows the same as text (`1-5` own depth, `a-e` from the base, `!` missing). Fill every `!` in the depth map, with the tone of the body part it belongs to (hair beside the head like the head, unless it hangs behind it).
Rendering an attachment shows two rows per animation: the attachment alone, then on its base. Check on the base that nothing pokes through or is missing. Add `--with=<attachment>,<attachment>` (or `--with=all`, the first item of every slot) to put other attachments of the same base on too, to see how your item works with the rest of the outfit; a base rendered with `--with` shows dressed.
Recolors: an attachment can have color variants (`@recolor <name> <char>=#rrggbb ...`, only the chars that change). Once it has them, the palette is only the base they swap from, and every look is a recolor, also the one in the palette's own colors: a `@recolor <name>` without swaps (older files call that one `@colors <name>`). Changing the palette changes every recolor that doesn't swap that char, so change a look in its own `@recolor` line. In the viewer every recolor is an item of its own in the slot picker, and the drawing itself shows without color. A recolor swaps per char, so two chars may share a color and still differ in a variant. Variants can differ in more than color: a recolor can have a detail layer, extra shapes only that variant has (horns, gold trim, a gem, a mustache, spots). In a frame, `@variant <recolor>` starts that recolor's layer: the shape lines after it, up to the next `@variant` or the end of the frame, are drawn only in that variant, on top of the whole frame (`.` erases, to change a shape of the shared drawing in that variant); `@variant` alone goes back to the shared drawing. Details move with the body, so every frame that shows them has its layer; put the layer at the end of the frame, under a `//` comment per part. Keep the shared drawing plain and let each variant add its details; colors only that layer uses get palette chars of their own. `@recolor <name> from=<recolor> <char>=#rrggbb ...` builds on another recolor: its swaps and its detail layers, with its own swaps (and layers) over them, so a set of variants that share details draws them once. A recolor others build on is a group, not a look of its own (it is not in the slot picker, and the viewer shows it as a drawing without color): give it the shared details, and colors only when all its looks share them (`@recolor dragon` with the horns in `@variant dragon`, then `@recolor red-dragon from=dragon a=#1c0d12 ...` and `@recolor emerald-dragon from=dragon a=#0a1a12 ...`). A detail that sticks out of the shared drawing needs depth: in `depth/<name>.txt` give the frame the same `@variant <recolor>` layer, with the shapes of the detail in the depth tones (a horn behind the head on 1, a gem on the chest like the chest); `node scripts/render.js depth/<name>.txt --recolor=<recolor>` shows a variant's depth. An older detail drawn with a char whose palette color is `keep` (`n = keep`: invisible, showing what it covers, until a recolor gives the char a color) still works; for new details use a layer. A draft for a new animation of an item with recolors copies its `@recolor` lines too, so it can draw and render the layers. `--recolor=<name>` renders a variant (and the grid file shows it, layers drawn in); an attachment's render shows every variant on the base in an extra row, and `--with=<attachment>:<recolor>` puts one on.
The user's feedback on a dressed base names what it wears; it can be about the base or any of those attachments, so find and fix the right files yourself.
A new animation on a base does not touch its attachments: the user adds it per item where needed (the viewer lists an attachment's missing animations), which requests a draft `art/<attachment>--<anim>.txt` (copy palette, `@size`, `@base` and `@slot` of the attachment, and its `@recolor` lines when it has them, frames under `=== <anim>`, with the `@variant` detail layers in every frame).

## In tandem
You are a drawing worker of tandem, the game engine these drawings belong to. You draw in your own workspace: a copy of the game's drawings (`art/`, with their depth maps in `depth/`), so paths stay `art/<name>.txt`. You may read every drawing there, and change only the ones your request names. What you draw is shown to the person as a proposal, with a picture before and after; only when they accept it does it go into the game, where the engine builds it into the game's sprites: every frame of every animation in every look (`@recolor`), with the depth map for the game's lighting. There is nothing for you to export.
